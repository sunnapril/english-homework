Liane

She has only a few hundred euros a month to live on. About half goes on rent, and she uses the rest for bills, food and meeting friends. She tends to stock up on essentials when a supermarket has a special offer. At the end of the month, she sometimes eats porridge for every meal.

Andrea

She admits that she is not good at living on a budget. She tends to splash out on gadgets she does not need and then panics when she gets into debt. She tries to cut back on non-essential spending, but usually overspends again.

Omar

He is strict about budgeting. He takes out €80 each week and lives on that amount. If he borrows money, he pays it back as soon as possible. He also sets aside some money every month for emergencies.

What is a false economy?

Sometimes something looks like a bargain because buying it now appears to save money. Later it breaks, fails to work or is never used. The choice that seemed cheap finally costs more. Economists call this a false economy.

Explain false economy in your own words. Then give one real or realistic example of something that looked economical but cost more in the long run.

Mistakes in the past: forms that do different jobs

Compare the meanings before completing any exercises. Similar forms do not always express the same kind of regret or possibility.

wish / if only + Past Perfect — regret about something that did or did not happen. If only is usually more emotional, especially when only is stressed.

I wish I had taken the job with the higher salary.
If only I hadn’t taken out such a big loan.

should / ought to have + past participle — regret, advice after the event or criticism of a past action.

I shouldn’t have spent so much on my last holiday.
You ought to have cut back on your spending sooner.

could have + past participle has more than one use:

1 · an opportunity not taken: We could have bought a bigger car, but we didn’t want to spend too much.

2 · an evaluation of the actual result: It could have been worse. (= It was not so bad.) · I couldn’t have done it better myself. (= You did very well.)

3 · something was impossible without help: I couldn’t have done it without you. (= Your help made it possible.)

might have + past participle can express a similar unreal past possibility, often with an if-clause.

I might have got a better degree if I’d studied harder.

was / were supposed to + infinitive describes an expectation or arrangement. It can refer to something that did not happen or to something that happened but should not have.

I was supposed to make my first payment last week, but I forgot. · You were supposed to fix it, not break it!

What does the sentence actually tell us?

Choose the interpretation that follows logically from each sentence. Do not choose by recognising one grammar form; check what really happened.

1. I was supposed to arrive earlier than usual today.
Was the expectation fulfilled?
Correct answer: I didn’t arrive earlier than usual.
2. If only I had accepted my parents’ offer in 2009.
If only + Past Perfect describes the opposite of the past reality.
Correct answer: I didn’t accept my parents’ offer.
3. You shouldn’t have got into debt at university.
Is the speaker approving or criticising the past decision?
Correct answer: I think this is bad.
4. I couldn’t have done better myself!
This fixed expression is a compliment: the result was as good as the speaker could achieve.
Correct answer: You did as well as me.
5. We might have been better off if we hadn’t invested in that tech company.
Better off if we hadn’t… means the actual decision left them in a worse position.
Correct answer: It was a bad investment.
6. They weren’t supposed to buy the motorbike, just test it!
A negative expectation does not mean the action failed to happen.
Correct answer: They bought the motorbike.
