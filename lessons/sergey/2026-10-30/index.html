Choose by noun and meaning

Total amounts: all (of), both (of), each, every
Large amounts: most, a lot of, lots of, loads of, plenty of, much, many
Medium/small amounts: some, several, a little/little, a few/few, a couple of
Zero or unspecified amounts: any, no, none of, either, neither
Countable or uncountable?

many / a few / few / several / a couple of + plural countable noun

much / a little / little + uncountable noun

A couple of normally means two, but informally it can mean approximately two or a very small number.

A useful register point

lots of and loads of are more common in informal speech. Plenty of means “more than enough”.

Much and many are especially common in negatives and questions, although many can also occur in positive statements.

Zero or an unspecified amount

Use no + noun but none of + pronoun/definite group: no evidence; none of the evidence.

Any and either are common in negatives and questions. Either and neither refer to exactly two.

With of

Use of before pronouns and definite groups: all of them, both of the suspects, most of the money, none of these keys.

Do not say every of them; say every one of them.

Small quantity, different attitude

a few / a little

Some exist; often enough for the situation.

We have a few leads, so the investigation can continue.
We have a little time before the interview.

few / little

Less than expected; often not enough.

We have few leads, so progress is slow.
We have little time, so we must hurry.

1. The detective found useful fingerprints, which gave the team a new lead.
The result is positive: there were enough to produce a lead.
Correct answer: a few
2. residents saw the car clearly, so the description remained vague.
The result is negative: there were not enough clear witnesses.
Correct answer: few
3. We still have time, so let us check the footage once more.
Some time remains and it may be enough.
Correct answer: a little
4. The witness gave useful information, and the police could not identify the suspect.
The information was insufficient.
Correct answer: little

All, every, both, either, neither or none?

all + plural/uncountable noun: all witnesses, all evidence · all of + pronoun/definite group: all of them, all of the evidence
every + singular noun: every witness — never every them
both / either / neither: exactly two · none of: zero members of a group of two or more
each or every?

Both refer to all members of a group and take a singular noun and verb. Every views the group together; each considers the members individually. Each can refer to two or more; every normally refers to more than two.

Every juror received the same document. · The judge addressed each juror by name.

Verb agreement

Each, every, either and neither take a singular verb. With none of + plural noun, both singular and plural verbs occur; the plural is now common.

Neither suspect was released. · None of the witnesses has/have reported anything.
